Prepare a round set

The game only works if the faculty passages are really written by people. Collect those first, then make AI versions of the same kinds of writing so the pairs feel alike.

  1. Ask four to six colleagues for one short piece each, 60 to 150 words: a late-work policy, a comment on a student paper, a reminder before an exam, a reply to a student email.
  2. Remove names, course numbers, and anything about real students.
  3. For each faculty piece, use the AI prompt below to write a matching AI piece of the same type.
  4. Paste everything into Edit passages, set each one’s author, and add a reveal note.
  5. Shuffle, then download the game file and upload it to your site.

Aim for 8 to 12 rounds, about 10 minutes. An uneven split, like 7 AI and 5 faculty, keeps people from counting.

Message to colleagues

Hi! For the AI teaching workshop I’m running an icebreaker called “AI or Faculty?” where people guess whether a short piece of teaching writing came from a person or from AI. Would you send me one short thing you wrote yourself, about 60 to 150 words? A syllabus policy, feedback you gave on student work, a course announcement, or a reply to a student email all work. I’ll remove names and course details, and I won’t say who wrote it unless you want credit. Thank you!

Prompt for the AI passages

You are a community college instructor. Write a [TYPE, e.g. late-work policy for a syllabus] for a [SUBJECT] course, about [WORD COUNT] words. Write it the way a real, busy instructor would: plain language, one or two personal touches, no headings or bullet points. Here is an example of the length and tone I want, written by a colleague:

Questions for afterward

  • Which round fooled the most people, and what made it convincing?
  • What clues did you rely on? Did they actually work?
  • If we can’t reliably tell, what does that mean for take-home writing assignments?
  • What would an assignment look like where it doesn’t matter whether a student used AI to draft?
