    Why AI graphics go wrong

    0:05 – 0:12

    Most problems come from asking one kind of tool to do another kind of tool's job.

    An image generator creates a picture pixel by pixel, the way it learned from millions of images. It's very good at scenes, textures, lighting, and style. But it doesn't write words; it draws shapes that look like letters. That's why labels come out misspelled, arrows point the wrong way, and numbers on a chart don't match anything.

    A chatbot can also write code that draws a graphic, usually as an SVG (a scalable vector graphic, the same kind of file many logos and icons use). Code places real text at exact positions, so every label is spelled the way you typed it and can be edited later. Code can't paint a realistic scene, though.

    Three ways to make a graphic

    Image with text

    The image generator draws everything, labels included.

    Best for scenes, illustrations, and banners with little or no text

    Image, no text

    Generate a clean picture, then add labels yourself in PowerPoint, Google Slides, or Canva.

    Best for labeled illustrations that need a drawn look

    Code-drawn graphic

    The chatbot writes SVG or HTML that draws the graphic with exact text.

    Best for diagrams, flowcharts, charts, timelines, and reference sheets

    Try it: image or code?

    For each graphic, choose how you'd make it. You'll get feedback right away.

    Choosing a tool and account

    0:12 – 0:17

    The prompts in this workshop work in any of these tools. What changes is your privacy protection and how much you can generate each day.

    Select the situation that matches your school.

    Your school uses Microsoft 365

    Your school email is a Microsoft account, so it can't sign in to Gemini, and ChatGPT isn't covered by your school's agreements. You'll use a personal ChatGPT or Gemini account. That works well for making course graphics, with a few habits to follow.

    • Check your school's AI policy first. Some institutions limit which AI tools employees may use for course work.
    • Keep it to shareable material. Use your own content, openly licensed resources, and public information. A personal account isn't covered by any school data agreement.
    • Never upload student photos, names, work, or records.
    • Deliver files, not logins. Download the image or SVG and post it in your LMS, OneDrive, or Teams. Students never need an account.
    • Don't require students to sign up. Asking students to create personal accounts with an AI company raises privacy and equity questions. Make it optional, or check with your institution.

    Your school uses Google Workspace for Education

    • Use Gemini with your school account if your administrator has turned it on. It's covered by your school's Google agreement.
    • Your admin controls access. If Gemini or image creation doesn't appear, contact your IT department.
    • Some features vary by plan and age. Higher-quality image models and some editing features may depend on your school's edition or on users being 18 or older.
    • ChatGPT needs a personal account, which isn't covered by your school agreement.

    Even with these protections, follow your school's rules about what may be used with AI, and never upload photos of students.

    Using personal ChatGPT or Gemini accounts

    • Both are free to start. Free plans allow a small number of images per day, and paid plans raise the limits. Limits change often and vary by model.
    • ChatGPT creates images with OpenAI's current image model (ChatGPT Images 2.0) and can open code in Canvas to preview it.
    • Gemini creates images with Nano Banana 2 by default. Paid subscribers can choose Redo with Pro, which Google says handles text and infographics in more detail. Gemini can also preview code in Canvas.
    • Treat both like any consumer service: don't upload confidential or personal information.

      Lab 1: an illustration from an image generator

      0:27 – 0:45

      Image generators are at their best when they draw the picture and you supply the words.

      In this lab you'll make a visual analogy or a labeled illustration with no text, refine it in conversation, then add labels yourself so every word is right.

      Generate the image

      1. In the prompt builder, choose your discipline, fill in step 1, pick Visual analogy or Labeled diagram, and choose Image, no text. Select ChatGPT as the tool.
      2. Copy the prompt and paste it into a new ChatGPT chat. ChatGPT recognizes that you want an image and generates it.
      3. Wait for the image to finish. It can take up to a minute.
      4. Open the image to view it full size, then download it.
      1. In the prompt builder, choose your discipline, fill in step 1, pick Visual analogy or Labeled diagram, and choose Image, no text. Select Gemini as the tool.
      2. In Gemini, select Create image from the tools in the prompt box, or just paste the prompt; it starts with "Generate an image."
      3. Gemini generates the image with Nano Banana 2. If you have a paid plan, the More menu offers Redo with Pro for more detail.
      4. Download the image from the menu under it.

      Refine it in conversation

      Don't start over when something's off. Stay in the same chat and ask for one change at a time. The AI keeps the rest of the image and adjusts only what you name.

      Fix one thing
      Keep everything the same, but make the background plain white and remove the decorative border.
      Remove stray text
      Remove every letter, word, and number from the image. Don't change anything else.
      Match a set
      Make a second image in exactly the same style, colors, and viewpoint as this one, showing [next stage or concept].

      The Revision tab in the prompt builder writes these follow-ups for you, and repeats your original spelling and accuracy rules so they aren't lost.

      Add your own labels

      1. Insert the downloaded image on a slide in PowerPoint or Google Slides.
      2. Add text boxes for each label, typed or pasted from your term list.
      3. Draw leader lines from each label to its part with the line tool.
      4. Group the image, labels, and lines so they move together, then right-click the group to save it as a picture if you need a single file.

      Hands-on lab: make and label an illustration

      About 15 minutes.

      Facilitator: Ask two or three people to show their before-and-after edits. The most useful discussion point is usually how specific the follow-up request had to be. If someone gets text in a "no text" image, use it as a teaching moment: the fix is a one-line follow-up.

      Lab 2: a diagram drawn with code

      0:45 – 1:02

      For anything with many labels, numbers, or arrows, let the chatbot write code instead of a picture.

      You'll ask for an SVG file. You don't need to read or write code: you copy what the AI gives you into a file, and the browser or your slide software does the drawing.

      Generate and preview

      1. In the prompt builder, keep your content from Lab 1 or start a new concept. Choose a Process, Cycle, Comparison, or Decision flowchart type, and choose Code-drawn graphic (SVG).
      2. Copy the prompt into ChatGPT or Gemini.
      3. If the result opens in Canvas, you'll see a preview next to the code. If not, ask: "Show this SVG inside an HTML page in Canvas so I can preview it."
      4. Read the list of labels the AI sends back, and check each one against your term list.

      Save it as a file

      1. Copy all of the code. Use the copy button on the code block so nothing is missed.
      2. Open a plain text editor: Notepad on Windows, or TextEdit on a Mac with Format > Make Plain Text selected first.
      3. Paste the code and save it with a name ending in .svg, such as water-cycle.svg. If your editor adds .txt, rename the file.
      4. Double-click the file. It should open in your web browser and display the diagram.

      If the file shows code instead of a picture

      The file was probably saved as rich text or with a .txt ending. Save it again as plain text with only the .svg ending, or ask the AI for a version that starts with <svg and contains nothing before it.

      Get it into your slides

      Choose Insert > Pictures and select the .svg file. It stays sharp at any size. To edit labels or colors directly in PowerPoint, right-click it and choose Convert to Shape.

      Google Slides doesn't accept SVG files, so convert the graphic to PNG first. Two easy ways: ask the AI, "Give me this as a PNG," or open the SVG in your browser and take a full-size screenshot. Keep the SVG so you can make changes later.

      Most LMS page editors accept an uploaded image. Upload a PNG for the widest support, or an SVG if your LMS allows it. Always add alt text when you insert it. Interactive HTML graphics are different: LMS editors usually strip their code, so host the file and link to it or embed it.

      Hands-on lab: draw a diagram with code

      About 15 minutes.

      Facilitator: Saving the file is where most people get stuck, especially on a Mac where TextEdit defaults to rich text. Demonstrate it once on the projector before people start. Have a finished .svg ready to share if someone's code won't render.

      Check it: accuracy and accessibility

      1:02 – 1:12

      An AI graphic looks finished long before it is. Checking is the part that makes it yours.

      Spot the errors

      This infographic is the kind of thing an AI tool might return. Read it closely, then mark every statement below that describes a real problem.

      Write alt text that teaches

      Alt text for a teaching graphic shouldn't just describe the picture. It should give a student who can't see it the same takeaway. Keep it short, and put the full detail in a long description below the image or linked from it.

      Long description

      The quick accessibility pass

      • Grayscale test: view it in black and white. If you can't tell parts apart, meaning depends on color alone.
      • Back-row test: project it or shrink it to phone size. If you can't read the labels, make them bigger or use fewer.
      • Contrast: dark text on a light background, or the reverse. Avoid text over busy parts of an image.
      • Text in images: any words baked into an image must also appear in the alt text or long description, because screen readers can't read them.
      Facilitator: Run the spot-the-errors activity as a room: give two minutes, then have tables call out what they found. Most groups miss the invented statistic, which is the point to emphasize: errors that look plausible are the dangerous ones.

      Copyright, provenance, and representation

      1:12 – 1:20

      Who owns an AI image?

      In the United States, the Copyright Office's January 2025 report on AI and copyrightability concluded that prompts alone aren't enough human authorship, so a purely AI-generated image generally can't be copyrighted. Your own contributions can be protected: the labels and text you add, and the way you select and arrange material into a larger work.

      • Others may be free to reuse a purely AI-generated image, so don't count on owning it.
      • A graphic you label, arrange, and combine with your own content has more of you in it, which matters for course materials you plan to share or publish as OER.
      • Your institution may have its own rules about AI content in course materials.

      This is general information, not legal advice. See the Copyright Office's AI page for the full reports.

      What not to ask for

      Living artists' styles

      Describe the look you want (line weight, palette, texture) instead of naming an artist.

      Characters and logos

      No cartoon characters, mascots, brand logos, or product designs, even "in a similar style."

      Real people

      No likenesses of real people, including colleagues, public figures, and especially students.

      Fake history

      Never present a generated image as a historical photo, document, or artifact.

      Provenance: how AI images are labeled

      Many AI images carry hidden signals that identify them as AI-made.

      • ChatGPT images include Content Credentials (C2PA metadata) and a SynthID watermark. You can check an image at openai.com/verify.
      • Gemini images include Google's SynthID watermark. You can upload an image to Gemini and ask whether it was made with Google AI.
      • Metadata is easy to lose: screenshots, editing, and some uploads strip it. Watermarks survive better but aren't perfect.

      Disclose it yourself

      Hidden labels aren't a substitute for telling students. A short caption such as "Illustration generated with AI and reviewed by the instructor" is clear and honest, and it models the transparency you want from students.

      How people are shown

      Image generators reproduce patterns from their training data, which can mean stereotyped professions, a narrow range of skin tones and body types, and assumptions about who is a nurse, an engineer, or a CEO. When a graphic includes people:

      • Ask for people of varied ages, genders, skin tones, body types, and abilities in realistic roles. The prompt builder adds this when you include people.
      • Look at a set of your graphics together. Patterns are easier to see across several images than in one.
      • In health and science courses, show conditions on a range of skin tones.

      Revise and share

      1:20 – 1:25

      The revision loop

      1. Check the graphic with the prompt builder's Checklist tab.
      2. Name the problems in the Revision tab, or type your own.
      3. Paste the revision prompt into the same chat. Fix one or two things at a time.
      4. Stop after two or three rounds if text keeps coming out wrong. Switch to a no-text image or to code instead of fighting it.

      Quick reference

      Take-away
      Glossary

      Image generator: an AI model that creates pictures pixel by pixel from a text description.

      Raster image: a picture made of pixels, such as PNG or JPG. It blurs when enlarged.

      SVG: a vector graphic described by code. It stays sharp at any size and its text is editable.

      Canvas: a side panel in ChatGPT and Gemini that shows code and a live preview.

      Leader line: the thin line connecting a label to the part it names.

      Alt text: a short text alternative read by screen readers in place of an image.

      Long description: a fuller text version of a complex graphic, placed near it or linked.

      C2PA / Content Credentials: metadata that records where an image came from.

      SynthID: an invisible watermark that marks content as AI-generated.

      Nano Banana: Google's name for the image models in Gemini.

      Troubleshooting

      Labels keep coming out misspelled. After two tries, switch to Image, no text and label it yourself, or use a code-drawn graphic.

      It won't make the image. Requests can be blocked for policy reasons, often because of real people, brands, or medical or violent content. Describe the concept more neutrally.

      A set of images doesn't match. Generate them in the same chat and ask for "exactly the same style, colors, and viewpoint as the last image."

      My SVG file shows code, not a picture. Save it as plain text with only the .svg ending.

      Google Slides won't take my SVG. Convert it to PNG first.

      The chart's numbers are wrong. Give the data in the prompt and ask for a code-drawn chart; never let it invent numbers.

      I hit a daily limit. Limits reset daily. Try the other tool in the meantime.

      It's blurry when printed. Use SVG or PDF for print, or ask for the largest available size.
