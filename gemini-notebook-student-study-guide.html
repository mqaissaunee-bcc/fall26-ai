    A self-paced tutorial for students

    Turn your class materials into a study kit Using Gemini Notebook, the tool formerly called NotebookLM

    Give Gemini Notebook your notes, slides, and readings. It answers questions from those materials and turns them into flashcards, practice quizzes, podcast-style audio, short videos, mind maps, and study guides. This page shows you how to make each one and, more importantly, how to study with them.

    1. GatherPut one unit's notes, slides, and readings in a notebook.
    2. AskChat with your materials until the ideas make sense.
    3. PracticeTest yourself with flashcards and quizzes.
    4. Fix the missesReview only what you got wrong, then test again.
    Time: about 45 minutes, or jump to what you need Cost: free with a Google account Current as of: November 2026

    Start here

    5 min

    A study tool that only knows what you give it.

    A general chatbot answers from everything it picked up in training, so its version of a topic may not match your course. Gemini Notebook works the other way around. You add sources (your notes, your instructor's slides, your textbook chapter), and it answers from those. Every answer carries numbered citations that take you to the exact passage it used.

    That matters when you study. The flashcards use your instructor's terms, the quiz covers what your class covered, and you can check anything that looks off.

    Google renamed NotebookLM to Gemini Notebook in July 2026. It's the same product, so older videos and guides that say "NotebookLM" still apply.

    What you need

    • A Google account. See Set up for which one to use.
    • Materials from one unit or chapter of one course. Start small.
    • A browser at notebook.google.com, or the Gemini Notebook app on your phone.

    Check your course rules first

    Making study aids from your own notes is usually fine, but AI rules differ from course to course. Read the AI policy in your syllabus, and ask your instructor if you're unsure, especially before uploading their slides or using AI on anything you'll turn in.

    Your first 15 minutes

    In a hurry? Do these six steps now and read the rest later.

    Set up your account

    3 min

    Which account you sign in with depends on what your school provides.

    Use a free personal Google account

    If your school email opens in Outlook, it won't sign in to Google tools. A personal Gmail account works fine.

    • Your school's privacy agreements don't cover a personal account, so treat it like any consumer app.
    • Skip the thumbs-up and thumbs-down buttons on anything private. On personal accounts, feedback can send that conversation to human reviewers.
    • You can share a notebook with classmates by email or with an "anyone with the link" setting.

    Use your school Google account

    If your school gives you a Google account, sign in with it. Your school's Google agreement covers what you upload.

    • If Gemini Notebook won't open, your school's administrator may have it turned off. Ask the IT help desk.
    • Public "anyone with the link" sharing is turned off. You can share with people at your school.
    • If an instructor assigns a notebook through Google Classroom, they may be able to see your chats in it. You'll see a notice when that's the case.

    What not to upload

    • Other people's private information: classmates' work, group chat screenshots, anything with names and grades.
    • Your own sensitive documents: financial aid forms, medical paperwork, ID numbers.
    • Materials you were told not to share: some instructors and publishers restrict uploading slides, exams, or paid textbook files. When in doubt, ask.
    • Lecture recordings made without permission. Ask before you record a class.

    Some features are limited to users 18 and older, including Cinematic videos, video visual styles, and voice conversations in the mobile app.

    Build a study notebook

    7 min

    A notebook is one project with its own sources, chat, and study materials. It has three panels: Sources on the left, Chat in the middle, and Studio on the right, where you make flashcards, quizzes, audio, and video.

    Add your sources

    1. Select Create new and name the notebook after the course and unit, such as "BIOL 101 Unit 3: Cell Division."
    2. Select Add sources, then upload files, paste a link, or paste text.
    3. Open each source once and read the automatic summary. If the summary is wrong or empty, the file didn't import well.
    What students can add as sources
    What you haveHow to add itTip
    Typed notesUpload the Word or PDF file, or paste the textYour own notes are the most valuable source. They show what your instructor stressed.
    Handwritten notesUpload a photo or scanWrite neatly and check the summary. Messy pages import poorly.
    Lecture slidesUpload the PowerPoint or PDFSlides are thin on their own. Pair them with notes or the reading.
    Textbook chapterUpload a PDF or EPUB of the chapterFree textbooks from OpenStax download as PDFs. Add one chapter, not the whole book.
    YouTube lecturePaste the linkThe video must be public and have captions.
    Recorded lectureUpload the audio fileIt's turned into a transcript. Record only with permission.
    Study guide or review sheetUpload itIt tells the tool what the exam is likely to cover.

    The free plan allows 50 sources per notebook and 100 notebooks, which is more than enough.

    One notebook per unit

    A notebook stuffed with a whole semester gives vague flashcards. A notebook for one unit gives specific ones. Make a new notebook for each unit or exam, and before finals, build one more that holds your notes and study guides from all of them.

    Use the checkboxes

    Every source has a checkbox. Only checked sources are used for chat answers and for anything you generate. To quiz yourself on Chapter 4 alone, uncheck everything else first. It's the simplest way to control what you get.

    Good in, good out

    The tool can only be as accurate as your sources. If your notes have a mistake, your flashcards will repeat it. Include at least one source you trust, such as the textbook chapter or the instructor's slides.

    Ask your materials

    7 min

    The chat is where you make sense of the material before you try to memorize it. Type a question in plain language and it answers from your checked sources.

    Check the citations

    Answers contain small numbered markers. Hover over one to see the quoted passage, or click it to jump to that spot in the source. Get in the habit of clicking a few. It catches errors, and rereading the original passage is itself good review.

    Turn on Learning Guide

    By default the chat hands you answers. Open Configure chat and choose Learning Guide, and it behaves like a tutor: it asks you questions, breaks problems into steps, and waits for you to try before explaining. Use it when you want to understand something, and the default style when you need a quick fact.

    You can also choose Custom and describe the tutor you want, for example: "Quiz me one question at a time on these sources. Don't give the answer until I try. If I'm wrong, give a hint first."

    Prompts worth copying

    Save what helps. Select Save to note under a good answer to keep it in the Studio panel with its citations. Your chat history is saved between sessions and stays private to you, even in a shared notebook.

    Pick the right tool

    3 min

    The Studio panel makes nine kinds of study material. Choose what you're trying to do and see where to start.

    Choose a goal to see a recommendation.

    Listening feels like studying. Testing yourself is studying.

    Audio and video are good for a first pass and for review on the go, but they're passive. What makes material stick is pulling it out of your own memory: answering a flashcard before you flip it, taking a quiz, explaining an idea out loud. Whatever you listen to or watch, follow it with a quiz.

    Flashcards

    5 min

    Best for vocabulary, definitions, formulas, dates, and anything you need to recall on demand.

    1. Check the sources you want cards from.
    2. In Studio, select the pencil icon on Flashcards.
    3. Choose how many cards (fewer, standard, or more) and a difficulty (easy, medium, or hard).
    4. Add a prompt that says what to focus on, then generate.

    How to study a deck

    • Answer before you flip. Say it out loud or write it down. Recognizing the answer after you've seen it doesn't count.
    • Mark each card Got it or Missed it, and be honest about it.
    • At the end, choose Only cards you missed and go again until that pile is empty.
    • Use Explain on a card you keep missing. It opens an explanation from your sources.
    • Use Shuffle so you aren't memorizing the order.
    • Come back tomorrow. Your progress is saved, and a short session each day beats one long one.

    Take them elsewhere. The download button saves the deck as a CSV file, which you can open as a spreadsheet or import into a flashcard app such as Anki or Quizlet.

    Build a flashcard or quiz prompt

    Practice quizzes

    5 min

    The closest thing to the real exam, and the most honest measure of what you know.

    1. Check the sources the exam covers.
    2. Select the pencil icon on Quiz. Set the number of questions and the difficulty.
    3. In the prompt, describe the kind of questions you want. Quizzes can include multiple choice, short answer, and fill in the blank, so ask for the format your exam uses.
    4. Generate, then take it with your notes closed.

    Get more out of each quiz

    • Try before you use Hint. Struggling for a moment is part of how memory forms.
    • Read the explanation for every miss, and for any question you guessed right.
    • Ask about your results. After a quiz, the chat can discuss your latest results. Try: "Based on my last quiz, what should I review, and where is it in my sources?"
    • Make a new quiz, don't retake the old one. A second pass through the same questions measures your memory of the quiz. Generate a fresh one on your weak topics.
    • Raise the difficulty once you score well on medium. Hard questions ask you to apply ideas, which is what most exams do.

    Quizzes can be wrong

    Now and then a question has a wrong answer key or two defensible answers. If something disagrees with your notes, check the citation, then your textbook, then ask your instructor. Spotting a bad question means you know the material.

    Audio Overviews

    5 min

    A podcast about your own course materials, ready in a few minutes.

    Select Audio Overview in Studio and two AI hosts talk through your checked sources. Select the pencil icon first to choose a format, a length, a language, and a prompt.

    Deep Dive

    Two hosts explain and connect the ideas. Use it for a first pass through a chapter or a review before the exam.

    The Brief

    One speaker, under two minutes. Use it right before class or as a warm-up before a study session.

    The Debate

    Two hosts argue opposite sides. Use it for courses where you have to weigh positions, such as ethics, history, or policy.

    The Critique

    Two hosts review the material like an editor would. Add your own draft or notes as a source to hear what's unclear or missing.

    Tell the hosts what you need

    Ways to listen

    • Download it to your phone so it plays without a connection.
    • Change the speed from the More menu on the player.
    • Interrupt the hosts. On a new Deep Dive in English, select Interactive mode, press play, then Join, and ask a question out loud. They answer from your sources and pick up where they left off.
    • Choose another language. Audio Overviews work in more than 80 languages, which helps if you think more easily in a language other than the one your course is taught in.

    Make it active

    Pause every few minutes and say the last point back in your own words. When it ends, write down three things you remember without looking, then take a quiz on the same sources.

    The free plan allows three Audio Overviews a day, so use the prompt to get what you want on the first try.

    Video Overviews

    4 min

    A narrated video with diagrams and key points, built from your sources.

    Video helps most with things that are easier to see than to hear: processes with steps, cycles, structures, cause and effect, and comparisons.

    Explainer

    A structured walkthrough of the ideas. This is the one to use for studying.

    Short

    About 60 seconds on the key concepts, in a vertical format for your phone. Good as a quick refresher.

    Cinematic

    An animated, story-style video. English only, ages 18 and up, and on paid plans.

    1. Check only the sources for the concept you're stuck on. A narrow video is a better video.
    2. Select the pencil icon on Video Overview and choose Explainer.
    3. In the prompt, name the concept and say what confuses you.
    4. Generate, then go do something else. Videos can take 30 minutes or more and keep building if you close the tab.

    Start it early

    Because videos take a while, start one at the beginning of a study session and work through flashcards while it builds. The free plan allows three a day.

    Watch with a pencil in hand. Pause on each diagram and try to redraw it from memory. On-screen text and visuals are AI-generated and occasionally contain errors, so compare anything surprising with your sources.

    More study tools

    4 min

    Mind Map

    A branching diagram of the unit's topics. Expand a branch to see subtopics, or click any node to ask the chat about it.

    Study move: before you open it, sketch your own map of the unit on paper. Then compare. The branches you left out are what to study next.

    Study guide

    Select Reports, then the Study Guide template, for a written guide with key terms and review questions. Select the pencil icon to add instructions first, such as "include a glossary and five short-answer questions with answers at the end."

    Reports also offers an Interactive option that builds a Learning Overview you click through on the web.

    Study move: answer the review questions in writing before you read the answers.

    Infographic

    A one-page visual summary you can download as an image. Choose the level of detail and the orientation. Portrait works well as a phone lock screen during exam week.

    Study move: proofread it against your notes. Generated images sometimes misspell or mislabel things, and finding the errors is good review.

    Data Table

    Pulls facts from your sources into a comparison table. Ask for the rows and columns you want, such as "compare the four tissue types by structure, function, and location." It exports to Google Sheets.

    Study move: copy the table with one column left blank and fill it in from memory.

    Slide Deck

    Builds slides from your sources. Useful for a study group, where each person presents one topic.

    Study move: teaching is one of the best ways to learn. Present the deck to a friend, or to an empty room, without reading from the slides.

    If you use generated slides for a graded presentation, check your course's AI policy first and say how you used the tool.

    Study with other people

    Select Share to add classmates by email. Everyone can chat with the same sources and use the same flashcards and quizzes, and each person's chat stays private. A study group can split the work: one person adds notes, another makes the quiz, a third checks it against the textbook. Share only materials you're allowed to share.

    On your phone

    The Gemini Notebook app for Android and iOS syncs with the website. Flashcards, quizzes, and audio all work there, and the app can record audio straight into a notebook. Some Studio options are web-only, so build on a computer and review on your phone.

    Study smart, stay honest

    4 min

    It makes mistakes

    Answers come from your sources, which cuts down on errors but doesn't remove them. Click citations, and trust your textbook and instructor over the tool.

    It only knows your sources

    If something from class isn't in the notebook, it won't be in your flashcards. Keep the notebook up to date.

    Do the reading anyway

    A summary tells you what a chapter says. It can't give you the practice of working through it. Read first, then use the tools to review.

    Watch for false confidence

    After a smooth podcast, everything feels familiar. Familiar isn't the same as known. A closed-notes quiz tells you the truth.

    Studying versus doing your work for you

    Uses that are usually fine and uses that need permission
    Usually fineCheck with your instructor first
    Flashcards and quizzes from your own notesUploading instructor slides, exams, or paid materials
    Asking for an explanation of a concept you're stuck onGetting feedback on a draft you'll submit
    Audio or video review of a chapter you've readUsing generated slides, text, or images in graded work
    Practice problems with worked stepsUsing it during an open-note quiz or take-home exam

    Copying generated text into an assignment and turning it in as your own is a different thing from studying, and at most schools it's an academic integrity violation unless the instructor has allowed it. Your syllabus is the authority. If it doesn't say, ask.

    Check yourself

    3 min

    Seven questions on what you just read. You get feedback as soon as you answer.

    Quick reference

    Keep handy
    Something went wrong

    My flashcards are too general. Uncheck unrelated sources, and name the topic in the prompt.

    A source imported as gibberish or blank. Scanned or photographed pages often fail. Try a clearer photo, a typed version, or pasting the text.

    A YouTube link won't add. The video needs to be public and have captions.

    The chat says it can't find the answer. It isn't in your checked sources. Add the reading that covers it, or check that the right boxes are ticked.

    There's no Join button on my audio. Interactive mode needs a newly generated Deep Dive in English.

    My video is still generating. That's normal. Close the tab and come back.

    I hit a daily limit. It resets tomorrow. Flashcards and quizzes have the most room, so use those.

    My school email won't sign in. Microsoft school accounts don't work with Google tools. Use a personal Google account.

    Words to know

    Notebook: one project with its own sources, chat, and study materials.

    Source: a file, link, or text you add. Answers come only from checked sources.

    Citation: a numbered marker linking an answer to the passage it came from.

    Studio: the panel that makes flashcards, quizzes, audio, video, and more.

    Learning Guide: a chat style that tutors you with questions and steps.

    Audio Overview: an AI-generated spoken discussion of your sources.

    Video Overview: an AI-generated narrated video of your sources.
